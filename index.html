4 · Measure the floor

Track = tire centre to tire centre across the axle. A straight edge shorter than the track is fine — centre it between the tires; a slope is a slope whether you read it over 48″ or 55″.

No floor slope stored.

Do the two blocks have to be exactly the same thickness?
If they’re taped to the straight edge: no, not even close. Turning it end for end swaps which block sits on which spot, so a mismatch tilts the first reading one way and the second reading the other way by the same amount, and the pair averages it out — the same trick that already kills the phone’s own zero error. That is the whole reason to attach them.

If you leave the blocks loose on the floor and just flip the edge onto them, the mismatch stays put and becomes part of the answer: Tape them on and stop thinking about it.

Measure the rear-axle line too if you’ll be doing rear camber. Re-measure whenever the vehicle gets rolled to a new spot.

Why this step, in detail

The fix for a floor that isn’t level. A sideways slope leans the whole vehicle, which shifts every camber reading. Caster and toe don’t need this — the way they’re measured cancels floor slope out.

What tilts the truck is the height difference between the two tire contact patches — not how the concrete happens to slope at any one spot. So you bridge straight across between the tires on a long straight edge that only touches at its two ends, and read the slope of that. High spots in between can’t lift it and dips in between can’t fool it.

How to hold it, and why three readings

Phone placement: bar upright on the wheel lip (feet at 12 and 6 o’clock), screen facing OUT — away from the truck — top of phone UP. Same hold as the camber leg of a caster run, so nothing has to move between them. Tap the wheel you’re on first so the live number uses the right side, hold still, capture when the steady light is green. The plumb dot below the readout only watches side-to-side twist — leaning the bar forward/back is the camber reading itself, so plumb won’t (and shouldn’t) react to that; it flags if you’re holding it rotated sideways.

Take three or four readings per wheel, lifting the phone off the bar and re-seating it between each one. Every tap adds a reading rather than replacing the last, and the tile shows the average, how far the readings scattered, and how tight that average is. Holding the phone by hand means each placement sits a little differently — that scatter is the real error here, and one reading can never show it to you. Tapping again without re-seating measures nothing new.

Other sweep modes, stop-angle tape calibration, sensor check

Steering sweep

Caster is not readable standing still — it shows up as camber change while you steer. Every mode here is the same measurement; they differ only in where the steer angle comes from.

Steering-stop angles — measure once with a tape
Do this once per vehicle. It replaces the phone’s steer-angle sensor entirely, which is the thing that has been wrong.
  1. Front wheels dead straight. Lay the straight edge flat against the rim flange, front and rear — not the tire sidewall, which bulges. It should stick out past the tire at both ends.
  2. Chalk a line on the floor along the edge. That line is your zero.
  3. Steer fully to the left stop. Re-seat the edge flat on the rim the same way.
  4. Measure the perpendicular distance from the chalk line to each end of the edge — front end and rear end. At full lock they land on opposite sides of the line.
  5. Repeat at the right stop. Enter all four numbers.

You don’t have to put the edge back in the same spot on the rim. The wheel pivots about the steering axis, not about the middle of your edge, so the edge slides sideways as it turns — and the difference between the two end offsets cancels that out completely. Flat against the rim is the only thing that matters.

A quarter-inch of slop in those measurements is about half a degree of stop angle, and half a degree of stop angle is about 0.05° of caster. The tape is roughly five times more accurate than this needs — which is the whole point of getting the angle off a tape instead of a sensor.
Tape the phone to the wheel sideways — long axis pointing front-to-back, not up-and-down. The steer angle is read off the compass, and steering pivots the wheel about a near-vertical axis: mounted upright that axis runs straight through the phone’s own top, so the top keeps pointing at the sky and the compass has no horizontal direction to report. Turned sideways the top swings through the horizontal plane by exactly the steer angle. Camber reads identically either way — it only uses the tilt of the screen, which doesn’t change when you rotate the phone in its own plane — so the sideways mount is free. The app checks which way round it is and says so; Check steer sensors proves it on your phone in about thirty seconds.
Engine off, brake held hard the whole sweep — a helper or a board wedged from the seat to the pedal. Front tires on something slippery (two vinyl tiles with grease between, or a pair of plastic bags each) so they can pivot.

Tires won’t turn far, or the reading looks wrong?
Any swing past ±8° is a valid sweep. If the tires won’t pivot far enough to reach the stops — not slippery enough, or the brake creeping — just steer as far as they will go, stop, and hold. It captures wherever you stopped and uses that exact angle. A small swing isn’t wrong, only noisier: at ±8° expect roughly twice the scatter of ±15°, so take more sweeps to make up for it. If caster comes out around −3° when +3° is expected, the turn direction is flipped on your phone: use Flip turn sign in Setup → Sensor details and re-run the sweep.
Nothing beeping? The phone’s ringer switch mutes the web-audio guidance on some iPhones — flick it off silent, or turn Voice off and watch the dial instead.
Caster wandering run to run — 3° one time, 6° the next, with the app insisting the captures agreed — is the signature of a bad steer angle, not of scatter. Captures inside one run share the same bad angle so they agree perfectly; whole runs disagree because the angle changes between them. The cure is the tape-calibrated sweep above. If you are using a sensor mode and seeing this, switch.
Caster several times too big (14° where 4° belongs) is the same disease further along: caster divides by sinθ, so an angle read three times too small comes back three times too large.
Set Auto-Lock to Never (Settings → Display & Brightness) before a sweep. The web wake-lock does nothing on iPhone, and once the screen sleeps the sensors stop.
Open Captures from the last run below after a sweep — it lists every angle and camber the app actually recorded, which is what to read out if a number still looks wrong.
SAI (steering-axis lean, small print under each result) is a free diagnostic: if one side differs a lot from the other with normal camber, suspect a bent spindle or strut.

How the carry works

Phone placement: bar laid across the wheel, flat, resting on the rim front and rear, screen UP, top of phone toward the front bumper on every wheel — the same hold as the caster heading leg. The gyro carries the pointing angle from wheel to wheel; revisiting the first wheel at the end measures the sensor’s drift so it can be subtracted. The gyro reads rotation about gravity, so it is indifferent to how the phone is rolled; the level target is there so every stop is held the same way. Carry the bar smoothly — no flips, no spins.

Toe tolerances are about a tenth of a degree — the edge of what a phone gyro does. Run 3 passes and trust the average; sanity-check with the tape method before final tightening.

Frame centerline (optional cross-check)

A truck's frame rails are manufactured straight and parallel to its true centerline — the same reference collision/frame shops use to check for impact damage. Pressing the phone against a straight, undamaged section of each rail and comparing headings gives a second, independent "straight ahead" — separate from the rear axle, useful for catching a subtly bent frame or an offset rear axle before you trust the thrust line completely.

Pick the front and rear frame check-points — usually the flat rail sections just behind the front bumper and just ahead of the rear bumper — because on a Sonoma-style ladder frame those are normally reachable from outside the truck, no jack stands needed. Avoid any kicked-up, notched, or visibly bent section.

No gauge bar needed here — case off, hold the bare phone flat against the rail's outer face, screen up, top of the phone pointing toward the truck's front, same as a wheel capture.

Run + Frame rails mode above. It measures each rail front-to-rear first (a built-in straightness check — a bent rail flags itself before it can mislead you), then reports the frame centerline against the thrust line already captured in the same pass.

Center the steering wheel

The trick: the rear axle defines the direction the vehicle actually travels (the thrust line). The fronts must be set square to that, not to the body. All-4 passes measure each front wheel against the thrust line, so:

  • Strap the steering wheel dead center (bungee through the rim to the seat frame) and keep it there for all measuring and adjusting.
  • Run 2–3 all-4 passes. Results shows LF and RF toe individually vs the thrust line.
  • Adjust each side’s tie rod until LF and RF each read half the total-toe target. Equal halves = wheels square to the thrust line = steering wheel stays centered driving straight.
  • If a test drive still shows a cocked wheel: note which way, then move both tie rods the same small amount in the same steering direction (e.g. wheel cocked left → lengthen right rod, shorten left rod equally on a front-steer rack — small steps, re-drive).

Tape toe (trustworthy cross-check)

Mark the middle tread block on each tire of one axle at hub height, front and rear of the tire. Measure across the vehicle: front-of-tire marks, then rear-of-tire marks (roll half a turn if the frame blocks the tape — same marks). Toe-in = rear wider than front.

Mark spacing = front mark to rear mark on one tire (≈ tire diameter if marks are at hub height).

Build the gauge bar

One straight bar, two bolt feet, rubber straps for the phone. Half an hour, ~$20. One bar fits all three vehicles if you slot the foot holes or drill two sets.

Materials

  • Bar: aluminum flat bar or rectangular tube, 1″–2″ wide, 20–24″ long. A cheap 24″ aluminum level works great. Straightness only matters at the two feet, not the middle.
  • Feet: two 5/16″ bolts, 1½″ long, each with two nuts (jam-nut). Space them so the bolt tips land on the wheel’s machined rim lip — the ring where clip-on weights go (≈15½″ apart on the Sonoma’s 15s; wider on the Cayenne/Highlander alloys — slot the holes or drill a second set). Never on the tire; rubber bulges.
  • Phone mount: two heavy rubber bands or a velcro cinch strap around bar and phone (case off, back flat against the bar). It must not shift between captures.

Why it works even if built sloppy

The two bolt tips define the measuring line — the bar doesn’t have to be perfect, and unequal foot lengths get canceled by the gauge-zero step. Only two things matter: feet on clean metal lip, and the phone strapped so it can’t move.

Why the screen direction keeps changing. Camber is gravity along the screen normal, so that axis has to point out through the wheel — and rolling the phone about it changes nothing, which is why camber never cares whether the top is up or sideways. A compass heading is built from the two horizontal axes, so the phone has to lie flat: that puts the screen-normal axis vertical and out of the answer, which matters because on a wheel it is aimed straight through the cast-iron brake rotor. Tilt it 2° and 3.5% of that iron leaks back into the heading; hold it against the wheel face and you get 100% of it. The gyro is the odd one out — blind to magnets and to roll — so the toe carry only cares where the feet sit and which way the top points.

The two that look wrong but are not. Gauge zero step 3 turns the screen toward the wheel on purpose: flipping it is how the routine separates your phone’s built-in lean from the bar’s. And the caster heading leg is the only capture where turning the phone end-for-end matters — it reads as a 180° steer and the run is refused. Everywhere else, which way round it sits is free.

Day-of checklist

  • Tires at equal, correct pressure. Normal load in the vehicle.
  • Park on the flattest spot you have, wheels straight. Bounce each corner and roll a foot forward to settle.
  • Setup tab top-to-bottom: vehicle → sensors → flat check → gauge zero → floor.
  • Measure everything before wrenching: camber all 4 → caster sweeps → toe passes.
  • Adjust in this order: caster → camber → toe. Toe always last — everything else disturbs it.
  • After every adjustment: drop the jack, roll a foot, bounce, re-measure. Never trust a reading taken right off the jack.
  • Steering wheel strapped centered for all toe work.
Safety: measuring happens wheels-on-ground, but adjusting means loosening suspension bolts — chock the wheels, and never any part of you under a vehicle held only by a jack.

What the floor trick actually does

Camber is judged against gravity, but the spec assumes level ground. If the floor drops ½° toward the passenger side, the whole vehicle leans that way: the gauge reads the passenger wheel ½° more positive and the driver wheel ½° more negative than truth. The floor step measures that lean along the axle line (a flipped pair of captures, so the phone’s own flat error cancels) and the app adds it back with the correct sign per side — separately for the front and rear axle spots.

Caster is immune (computed from camber differences during the sweep — the lean sits in every reading equally). Toe never references gravity at all. Front-to-back slope barely matters for any of the three.
